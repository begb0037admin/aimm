Again, this might have already been fixed for you, but if it hasn't, you want to make sure that you fix that before you dive into the rest of the mix. And that alone will make your overall mix a lot more cohesive and punchy. So, sticking with our approach of treating our drums as one whole instrument, I would send all of my drum channels to a drum bus. Not only will it allow me to control the level balance of my instrument group very easily, but it will also allow me to process my drums as one whole instrument. Now, if I have elements that have several channels like the snare top and bottom microphone or if there are a couple of microphones on my kick drum as well as samples, then I would usually create submixes for those elements so that when I'm applying process into my snare, I'm applying it to all the snare channels, including any samples that there might be. Now, at this point, I would also pan my drums to achieve an accurate stereo image. It's totally up to you whether you go from the audience perspective as if you're facing the drummer and watching them or whether you go from the drummer's perspective as if you're sitting behind the kit. We have another blog on panning your drums that details these moves more specifically, but essentially, you want to pan your overheads left and right, leave the kick and snare in the middle, and then pan all the other elements accordingly. For example, your toms slightly to the right if you're going from audience perspective and your floor toms to the left, etc. . The level balance of your drums actually has a big impact on your sound. So, the next thing I would do is balance out my individual elements to achieve the drum sound that I want to or get as close to it as possible. At this stage, ideally, we don't want to be hitting anywhere close to or definitely not over zero DB on the drum bus or also on the master bus. Your overall mix should be hitting no higher than -6 dB and -3 dB so that you also leave a lot of headroom for mastering. So normally, I would do this at the start of every mix, is balance out my individual elements so that I can listen to a really great rough mix and get to know the song before I dive in. And it will also give me headroom for any processing that I apply. With balancing out my drum elements, there is no right or wrong way to approach that. I might be starting with bringing up my overheads to get a clear picture of the overall drum kit and then bringing up the close mic elements, or I might be starting up with bringing up my kick and snare and then introducing the rest.

EQ can actually get you very far along in this process, and we do have a free Ozone 11 EQ that will be great to start with. For example, you could try a de-esser on some harsh hi-hats, you could use an EQ to tame some ringing on a snare, but I also like to use tools like transient shapers to shape the drum further. The genre that you're working in will also help to identify what would be classed as a problem to you. At this point, you might want to gate certain elements like your snare or toms to eliminate some of the bleed from the rest of the kit, and that will work great in genres like pop where you want to keep things quite clean and polished, but you may want to keep some of that bleed in for genres like jazz where you really want to capture the ambience of the room and the kit and the overall sonics of the space that it was recorded in. Now that we have fixed any issues with our drum mix, you can start applying processing to the drum bus. There can be many different elements on your drum bus, so that could be saturation, it could be reverb, or you might want to create a separate bus for your reverb, but the two elements that are really consistent for me are EQ and compression. EQ will help us to balance out the frequency spectrum of our drums, and compression will help us to glue our drums together. And you really don't want to obliterate the dynamics of your drums, but rather tame and enhance them with compression.
