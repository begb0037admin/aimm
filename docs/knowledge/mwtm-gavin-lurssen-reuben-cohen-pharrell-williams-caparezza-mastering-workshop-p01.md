## Chunk 1

We're Lurson Mastering in Los Angeles, California, and today we're shooting with Mixed with the Masters. We're going to be going over our philosophies behind the process of mastering, working on some songs, show you how we do our thing, and I'm Ruben Cohen. And I'm Gavin Lurson. Let me give you a lowdown on the console we use. So we're mostly analog processing in this world. We do have the use of plugins as desired, but for the most part, we're working on a mix. We're clocking it, sending it into the console, and the chain we like to use for the most part is comprised of 2BQ and solid state EQ before it goes into any type of limiting or anything like that. So we have the flexibility to change the patching however we like it, but what we generally like to do is start the chain with a tube equalizer. And most of the time we use an EAR A25 diverse EQ. It's got wide and narrow bands. It always does the trick for us. It always offers some saturation on any type of digital mix that helps us end up with something very cohesive, very glued together. But it is dependent on everything else in the chain too. When we first decode the mix, the mix is coming into the EAR EQ before it actually hits the console. And the reason we do that is because when you look at what's later in the chain with some limiting and de-essing and high-frequency limiting, it works very well with the saturated 2BQ on the front of the chain. And then we put some things in the middle of the chain for very light processing to add up to something that's holistic and creates a hole that's greater than some of its parts. So if you bring in the mix, if you're decoding the digital mix and you're clocking each individual mix for what it came in at and going through the interface into the conversion and coming into the EAR, I'll explain when we work in some music how we use the EAR in this regard. But we then go into our console, which is a passive device, and we generally attenuate by a dB or so before it goes into the next device, which is usually the solid static cue. And we like this GML 9500. It's been with us for a very long time. It's got some very, very intricate bands that we can get into, some highly adjustable cues. And we use it mostly in the low end of the music and how it combines with the 2BQ. So once we have our EQ set, which 2BQ, and then a little bit of attenuation, and then into a solid state EQ, then we start deciding what kind of limiting that we need. If we decide we need some tube limiting in any type of production that we're working on, we'll generally patch it right on the backside of the GML.

That'll be the first place that we'll insert a tube compressor or limiter. So the Manly is a favorite. The Shadow Hills is a favorite. A Fairchild is also a favorite. There's many options out there. And if we have to wheel something in on another cart, we have the ability to patch it in as we go here. We'll go from tube limiting into either de-esser or high-frequency limiter. So what you see in our console here is a Masselec de-esser, and that is a very beautiful high-frequency limiter. And this is what's working in conjunction with the tube EQ on the front end of this stage. So if we decide to EQ some high-end or mid-range or shelf into the music, but we don't want it to be brittle and open, this de-esser or high-frequency limiter will be on the backside of the chain to clamp that down a little bit. And from there, if necessary, we'll go into just a nice peak limiter. In the console here, we have an Allen Smart C1, SSL, same thing. And there's many options for that. And this provides us a very cool chain because it allows us to open up the music using a tube EQ, getting some saturation in addition to some opening up EQ. A lot of times, people leave mixes for us to really crack them open, get them breathing. They prefer us to do that in the analog domain than on a digital plug-in. So very often times, people are expecting us to do that. And that saturation, with the addition of opening it up, and then some peripheral processing, and then at the end, a clamp down on the high end, really helps us to open it up and make it sound warm and open. So it's like a mid-range openness, but it's got some breath, but at the same time, it's not cutting your head off, you know? So this is a basic rundown of our chain. We have the flexibility to patch anything in any order. If something is not working, we can change where the de-esser goes. We can eliminate it. We can use only a 2BQ. Sometimes clients will come in, and they'll want only a solid-state EQ. They want what the color that the 2BQ offers. So it's all very dependent on who it is you're working with, what music it is you're working with, what genre you're working with. And there's people that span all the generations in the music business, too. And people that were working actively in the 1970s and even the 60s come in here and want a much more purist approach. And because we are an unbalanced, single-ended room, we have the ability to drive long cables without the use of op-amps, so we don't degrade the signal.

## Chunk 3

We can get very, very pure with this console and the way we are allowed to patch things. However, most of the time, this basic rundown really, really works. Each mix is clocked with an antelope clock. All of this gear, even the digital gear, is designed to be complementary to every other piece of gear. So any plug-in we might use, any interface we use, any clock we use, has a tonal characteristic that integrates with every other piece of gear. So for our signal chain, it's antelope clocking all throughout. Antelope on the playback mix, antelope on the capture mix as well. We use benchmark converters because we shot out a whole bunch of converters and decided that the characteristic of the benchmark in the way that we have modified them to feed this unbalanced, single-ended environment worked perfectly, didn't have any technical issues, no buzzes or anything like that. But there's something about the punchiness factor in these converters as we are trying out different converters. There are many converters out there that our colleagues use that they swear by. And when we try them on this console, they might sound a little soft, soft meaning a little loose in the low end, any type of characteristic, a little dark, a little less open. And then I'll go to their environment and I'll hear them and it'll sound magnificent. So every piece of gear as a design in a signal chain is designed to work in this environment as one holistic piece. So we look at this console as one fancy compressor EQ. And when you break it down, which is what we're doing, you can see how it all integrates with each other and the thought that went into why each piece needs to be what it is. So we do have the option of swapping them out and changing them as needed. And maybe there is a converter for a particular album that will come into play. But for the most part, we know what kind of music is coming in and we know the basis of the starting point of what we're going to have in our console that day. We know how it's going to end up. The benchmark also offers us a flexibility on the face panel of altering the level and the feed to the console. Gain structure is a very important part of what we do. But before we had that ability, the benchmark gain structure was very useful to have on the front panel. The way we monitor our captured file, also through a benchmark and into our monitoring system, is set up at a unity gain so that once we get our levels and we print our levels and we listen to exactly what those levels are, we calibrate the playback of the file that we worked on before that as we're working on an album so that we can play it back at that exact same level as the song we're working on.

Sometimes we'll use an overstayer, which is a saturating tool. And what that will do is it'll actually do two things at the same time. It'll saturate and create a little bit of a natural smile face curve. So as that's happening, we'll EQ to compensate for that smile face curve. And in the end, you'll end up with something that's flat or relatively flat, but with all of that dimension, that texture, all those processes working together as one to end up in a place that feels balanced. And then from there, if it's still flat, we can then EQ to correct for balance in the mix. So all of that working together and then following into the other steps of the chain, as Gavin was explaining. The important part of all of this, if you break down the chain and you listen to what Ruben just described, and you then combine that with the front stage of it, which is the equalization part and how that EQs into any compression stage and then gluing it at the end, the important thing is to use very little of each processor, very, very little. So we find the best pieces of gear. We do some modifications on them, which generally is about cutting out electronics that don't need to be there, cleaning up the signal chain. That's usually what the modifications are, because the characteristics of the piece of gear are what we choose them for. So we don't need to modify that kind of thing. We audition them as to how they integrate into this long chain of events on essentially a stereo mix. So very little of each piece of gear to create a whole that's greater than the sum of its parts. And the most successful job we can do is if it sounds like we were never there. So, you know, when you consider the passive console and the design throughout with longer wires without the use of op-amps, that type of thing, it's all designed so that the listener can have a direct connection with the audio.
