# Mastering Workshop — Part 1: Professional Background Equipment Monitoring Loudness Converters Metering Dynamics

## Chunk 1

Hi, my name is John Greenham. I'm a mastering engineer today with Mix of the Masters. I'm going to show you four songs, two of them Billie Eilish songs, basically share all of my tips and tricks. I've been mastering for about 25 years now, starting at the beginning of the sort of digital audio era, which is probably about 1989, 1990, something like that. When I first got interested in mastering, I had worked at Bernie Grunman Mastering. The thing that I noticed was that he did this very small EQ change and the entire mix opened up. As soon as I saw that, I was like, that is really cool. You know, that's very interesting. I've been trying to do that ever since. But anyway, that's what got me interested in it.

## Chunk 2

Starting out with, you know, in the early days of digital audio with a Studer Diaxis system, I had a room with one of those, which was, you know, really the first sampling device. And I was just fascinated by it. I was never that interested cutting vinyl. I know that's kind of sacrilegious to say, but as soon as the digital audio thing came along, I found that really fascinating. The early systems were very slow by comparison to modern equipment. If you were doing a record that had a lot of fades, the fades would take about a minute to render. And if you had somebody that was particular about putting the whole thing together and having crossfades and stuff, you could spend about two or three hours sequencing the album and doing all the fades and stuff like that.

## Chunk 3

The first real mastering room I worked in was Paul Stubblebine's room at Hyde Street Studio C. And Paul had three Pacific Microsonics Model 2s, which are really great converters, actually. I mean, even though they're very old, they still sound really good. I used to have a couple of them, but I sold them because you can't get parts for them anymore. But he had a lot of esoteric audio research here and just a lot of really high-end stuff. So that's when I really started understanding what's going on. I mean, basically the thing about mastering, the skill actually is learning to listen, is listening. You've got to understand what's the difference between clocking something this way as opposed to the other way. You know, who's the master, master clock, who's the slave, so on and so forth. All of those things are actually very important.

## Chunk 4

After that, I had a room with Steve Jarvis, who's a great supporter of Bay Area music scene. There we had the Massenburg gear, the Massenburg EQ, Massenburg compressor, which was also a pretty great sound, actually. Lastly, I worked at Coast Recorders. I had a room there, same kind of gear. And then I moved down here to Infrasonic, brought all my stuff down in a truck and started mastering records down in LA. Pete Lyman invited me to come down and work down here, which was great. After about four years at Infrasonic, I started working at home. So this is, you know, where I am now. It's the home mastering environment.

## Chunk 5

At first, I didn't think I would like it, but actually it's worked out really well. I'm using the key three speakers. The thing about them is that they're fairly room independent. They're designed so that the bass is focused forwards, so it doesn't bounce around, it doesn't build up in the corners and so forth. So they're ideal for an environment like this, you know, that isn't treated all that much. I mean, I just have panels on the sides to stop the sound from reflecting and a little bit of bass absorption. That's about it as far as treatment goes. Honestly, I prefer working in a room that isn't treated that much. I have had several experiences in the past with three different things where people spent a lot of money building rooms. You put all this thought into the dimensions and treating the walls with this and that and the other thing. And then you fire it up and it doesn't sound very good. And then you have to spend about a year tweaking it and putting bass traps all over the place. So I have not had that problem working at home. Actually, it's been quite straightforward, as a matter of fact.

## Chunk 6

When I met Phineas and Billie Eilish, that was a big turning point in my career. As their careers have taken off, then, you know, mine has also. That was a very good thing, getting involved with them.

## Chunk 7

My workstation is Sequoia, and then I have an RME, 8-channel RME card, AES. The AES out, I go out of 1 and 2 into the Lavery DA-N5, which is my D to A. The A to D is the master clock. Just running the audio through the chain makes everything clearer. I used to have, you know, the two-computer thing and different things, you know, different clocks. The atomic clock, the this, the that. But I like the sound that I've got at the moment better than just about anything, actually.

## Chunk 8

It also has several different noise shaping options. They're useful on particularly female vocal. It's kind of like an EQ, actually, without EQing anything. I never know what's going to work on a particular song. Sometimes you might think, oh, well, this will sound good with, you know, soft saturation. And you, you know, set it up, and it just doesn't. It's nice to have several high-quality options that you can go to, and you'll probably find that one of them will work. So the Lavery MX has, you know, enough possibilities to kind of suit lots of different things.

## Chunk 9

I know that some people always use the soft saturation on it, but I actually like the sound of it clean. I know that some people always clip them as well, but I, there again, I actually like the sound of it clean. I think it sounds really good that way. So I think, actually, it does go into clipping before it shows. It kind of, you can see it kind of flattening off the waveform. I think that's what, that's what that means. So there probably is some clipping, and indeed, you can clip it a little bit harder with some types of material. What happens is, is that then by the time I've captured it, it'll be within a dB or a dB and a half, maybe, of where it needs to be, which these days is not quite as loud as it was, you know, 10 years ago.

## Chunk 10

My sort of philosophy with these things is to keep the signal chain as simple as possible. I've worked in situations where there were switchers that you can kind of, you know, change volumes and compare things and take things in and out and all that. I don't like the idea of running through a lot of unnecessary wire. So I just have my D to A, and then it's, everything's connected in line. If you want to connect something a different way, you just get in the back and, and repatch it by hand.

## Chunk 11

## Chunk 16

The way I handle different levels of mixes, gain staging and so forth, is that the Lavery DA-N5 has a monitor output, which I assume that you're supposed to use for monitoring, although my monitoring is digital, so I don't use it for that. But I use that output and it has a very beautifully built attenuator on it, basically. It operates in steps of 0.25 dB. I usually leave whatever level the mix is at, I usually leave it at that level and then adjust so that it's hitting the analog chain exactly how I want it to. The Lavery MX is kind of, there's some gain in that as well. So it kind of comes in lower. And then I actually think the analog stuff, most stuff sounds better if you don't absolutely max it out. Although sometimes that can be good too.

## Chunk 17

Most people send a mix and then they send a loud reference that the client has been listening to. So that's kind of what you're usually aiming for as far as loudness goes. And generally speaking, that's pretty consistent. I mean, there's kind of a standard. The levels have come down a bit recently, I feel. I went through this thing with a lot of the producers that I work with a couple of years ago and I told them, you know, maybe we don't need to make things quite as loud because Spotify, you know, the normalization thing, it doesn't, you don't get any extra advantage of that sort of one or two dB of insanity that we always used to do with CDs. But nobody really paid any attention at that point. But recently I've noticed in the last probably three or four months, quite a few people have started talking about it and they're asking, they're saying, well, we want this to be a turn out at about minus 10 LUFS. There are people saying that now. So this is a big step forward, actually.

## Chunk 18

A lot of the old school big time mixers are still making stuff really, really loud. That's their sound and it's worked really well for them. They've mixed like all the hits that way. So there's nothing to argue about as far as that's concerned. But it is, I've noticed that some of the younger people are not, you know, they're sort of tuning into the fact that, you know, maybe you don't need to go quite to that extent. On the other hand, you know, sometimes you want the super loud, crazy sound. You know, that is what you want. The history of music is basically that. You know, you can get that by using, you know, a heavy compression. You don't have to have the overall level be so high. You can kind of achieve that kind of sound. You don't have to use limiting, you know, on the mix bar so you can get it other ways. It's more of an arrangement thing than anything else.

## Chunk 19

If your song starts with everybody playing and goes all the way through, like with no breaks, you're going to get punished by Spotify because the average level is going to be high. If in your arrangement there's some quiet parts, then the louder parts are going to be louder because they measure the average level of the song. So a good example of that is Billie Eilish's song, Zanny. And that's about the only thing they share. I'm in a secondhand smoke. Still just drinking canned coke. I don't need others any to feel better. Which has those super, super heavy bass parts in it. And then it has parts of the songs that are really whispery and low. So if you average it out, it's actually pretty close to the Spotify target level, even though the loud bits are really loud.

## Chunk 20

Sort of minus 10 is probably a good place for most songs. They have probably minus 8 during the loud bits and minus 12 during the not so loud bits. I mean, it's impossible to... Every song is different, of course. It comes down to making it sound good. That's kind of what it's all about. And if that's loud, then okay. If it's not quite so heavily limited, that's okay too.

## Chunk 21

I mean, honestly, I'm sort of more used to looking at RMS meters. So the RMS, the thing about the RMS is that it reacts more to the low frequency, reacts more to the bass. I feel like the LUFS measurement is more geared towards the mid-range. It's more geared towards the vocal. The way that you level match, for example, when you're mastering an album, the way that you match the levels of the songs is usually listening to the level of the vocal, because that's what people listen to. So, you know, song A, song B, if song A, the vocal's up here, and song B, it's down here, you try to match the level of the vocals. It doesn't matter if the music is not balanced, that's not so important. So it makes sense that I think that Spotify would be interested in that part of it. So, which explains why you can get away with songs that aren't turned down much or normalized on Spotify that have a lot of low end. The algorithm, I don't think, really cares about the low end that much. It's pretty much focused on a sort of higher frequency, it seems to me anyway.

## Chunk 22

So as far as delivering the files, I pretty much deliver everything at minus 0.3, unless I feel there's a lot of, you know, sort of true peak overs, or there's a lot of distortion on the track, and it's going to be a bit messy on Spotify, in which case I'll make a different set that's at minus 1. But usually minus 0.3, and that's the other point about the loudness thing actually, is that if you're just mastering and sending somebody one file, it's going to be in situations where it's going to be compared head-to-head with other songs in a non-Spotify environment. So you can't give them anything that's too quiet, really, because if they send it to a person for sync or something like that, then their song's really quiet compared to everything else the person's getting, and then it's not going to be good. So I don't usually worry about the true peak thing, honestly. These limiters that have true peak limiting, they actually sort of don't sound very good. You take your choice. You either have, like, inter-sample overs or, you know, it not sounding as good. And I'd rather, honestly, send out something that sounds as good as I can make it. That's my feeling about it.
