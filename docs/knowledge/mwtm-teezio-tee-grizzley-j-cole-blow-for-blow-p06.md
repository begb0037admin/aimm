# Blow for Blow — Part 6: Community Mindset Business Affairs

Source: MWTM set record: docs/mwtm/sets/Teezio_Tee_Grizzley_J_Cole_Blow_for_Blow_Mixing.md (local recording, no public URL)
Channel: Mix With The Masters

## Chunk 1

I think when we're talking about community, there's two types of communities. There's your initial inner circle that you come up with. And then there's the community of audio engineers as a whole, such as everyone that follows Mix with the Masters, everyone that goes to NAMM. So that's the much larger community. I think the importance with the larger community is allowing people that have figured out ways to do stuff or had enough experience in something to then be able to pass those things along to the next person. And without community, it's very hard. It's very hard for someone to learn from me because I'm not just going to let people come to my studio. But me doing something like Mix with the Masters allows a lot of people to see inside of the work that I do, usually in private and with clients. So I feel like nowadays with all the legends that you guys have here at Mix with the Masters, you guys are a huge part of the community and how the next generation of mixers and producers start to develop.

## Chunk 2

The importance of the community within your group of friends. I'll give you an example. Baines and I, Baines is an incredible mixer. He works with Young Thug and sort of in that realm of the music industry. And him and I are extremely close. We work in completely different ways. We couldn't be more opposite as mixers and how we work. But we still are able to end up mixing on the same album. We did Offset's album together. But the importance of that community, for example, is that there's a lot of stuff that Baines knows or uses that I don't. And it exposes me to those things. So it allows me to say, hey, I don't necessarily maybe like this about what he's doing, but this is interesting. And I think that there's a way that I can sort of make that apply into my setup and vice versa. There's a lot of things that I do that Baines might be like, oh, that's actually dope. Let me take that one thing and apply it into my situation. And I think that that can work as a community. You know, you have five or six mixer friends and all of you guys are sort of exchanging these ideas freely. It allows everyone to take from those ideas and apply it to their own situation. Versus if I was a mixer who had no community and no inner circle of friends, it would sort of just be up to me to develop myself and figure it out. But I wouldn't have any outside ideas for me to sort of play off of and sort of make my own.

## Chunk 3

I think that a lot of the stuff could be learned as far as the technical stuff like, oh, what compressor do I use? What EQ do I use? That's not something that you necessarily have to be in the room because at the end of the day, that EQ is just in the computer anyway. So whether you're learning it on YouTube or not is not as big of a deal. But I think that what someone can take away from being in the actual room is the etiquette of being in a studio, how to operate in a studio, how to deal with the staff, how to deal with the clients. So there's a lot more to learn from being in person than just the technical stuff.

## Chunk 4

I think someone can learn how to mix as far as sonically. But, you know, being a mixer, I operate with my business. You know, I have two businesses. I have a mixing business. I have my royalty business as well, which comes with the mixing. And so knowing so much more apart from just the mixing and being able to apply that is really the end goal because being a good mixer is not enough. You know, if you don't deal well with clients, if you don't take criticism well, if you don't have a team to, you know, chase your payments and you don't have a lawyer to make your, you know, your point contract for your royalty, like, you know, you're not going to be able to collect money. You're not going to get your points. Like, it's kind of useless to have just the technical ability and not the ability to actually make it a business.

## Chunk 5

I had a mentor, but he wasn't a mixer. It was a guy named Venus Brown. He was Black Eyed Peas manager for many years. And when he took me in to be underneath him, he was actually the chairman at BMG. So he sat right underneath, essentially, the CEO. And he didn't necessarily know, like, oh, you have to use this to go into this. But he knew how to EQ a vocal and he knew how to compress a vocal. He knew where things needed to be leveled to work. And that's where he mentored me in that sense of stuff. Even though this is a guy who went to the office every day and sat underneath the chairman, he was so good at his job that he was a studio guy. Like, he could come to the studio and literally vocal coach people. So that was my mentor, Venus Brown. And I always hit him and I just thank him for everything he taught me.

## Chunk 6

You know, I don't say yes when I don't want to say yes. Because a lot of times you commit to something. And one thing about me is if I commit, I'm committed. Like, I don't go back on it. And so I've learned that if I feel even an inkling of not wanting to do it, I won't commit to it because I won't want to have that feeling again of, like, be like, I don't want to do this, but I've already said yes. And I think in the beginning, everyone kind of has to say yes to everything because it's part of growing. You know, you never know what you're going to say no to and it could be the thing that changes your life. But after some time, you start to realize that your time becomes valuable and you need to put it towards the stuff that's really going to push you forward and not so much on stuff that, like, you know is not really going to go anywhere.

## Chunk 7

So I think focusing more on the important things now, back then, five years ago, I was just trying to mix any and every record that I could just to make sure that I had enough under my belt to be able to leave Chris Brown and recording Chris Brown. Because I was a recording engineer for him for many years and I needed to make that step outside of recording. I mean, I was, I had Industry Baby and First Class, two number one records, but I'm still recording Chris Brown, you know. So it was like figuring out how to get away from that and not have to record was the biggest jump. And part of that was sort of just saying yes to everything to be able to get me to wean off of having to stay recording for him and be able to fully function on my own. And then over time, I started to be able to like tighten it up and just say yes to the things that I want to be a part of. But that comes with time. You can't jump off a ship and then be a chooser. You have to, you know, get it on because beggars can't be choosers, right? So we have to just get what we can.

## Chunk 8

It doesn't matter what day, I'm in the studio every day. It doesn't matter if I'm doing notes or I'm actually mixing or we're just running stems and doing Atmos. Every single day we're in the studio without question. To me, it's the consistency. Every day I wake up at six or seven in the morning and I'm in the studio from that time until about 11. I go to the gym. That gives me a break to just decompress and then back to mixing. And that's every day for me, every single day. And it's just, that's the practice, right? Like maybe I'm mixing a record that's not for anyone important. That's me shooting shots at the gym, you know, because the record is for maybe a really independent artist from a small country or something like that. And it's like, it's not, you know, Chris Brown or anything like that. But those are still shots that I need to take to practice. So I feel like every single day I'm in the studio, that's my gym. And then I mix a really big record and that was the game, you know.

## Chunk 9

But every day is working and I have a schedule. I have my gym. I have the food that I eat. I usually work until essentially everything's done. If I get a day where I finish early, then I'll take the rest of the day off. And then what will happen is around 8 or 9, I'll be like, hey, maybe I should start one more song for tomorrow. But it's like, it's this addiction to having to do it because it's all I know. Like if I close Pro Tools, I don't know how to fix a computer if something happens. I know nothing about fixing computers. I know nothing about this console. I know nothing about how a speaker works. I just know that I know how to do that and listen. That's it. I get tired of all other types of stuff. I get tired of food. I get tired of driving the same car. I get tired of a lot of shit. But I don't get tired of mixing.

## Chunk 10

Thank you guys for joining me here in Paris. I want to thank the whole team at Mixed With The Masters for making this happen. And Audio Movers for helping along the way as well. And I hope you enjoyed it. Reporting live from Paris, France. Bye guys.
