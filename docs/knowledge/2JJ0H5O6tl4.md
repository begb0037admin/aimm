Have you ever had phase alignment issues or wondered what phase is? Well, let's quickly discuss what phase is, and then I'll show you how to easily address phase alignment issues using the new phase module in Neutron 5. Phase refers to the position of a sound wave relative to another at a given point. If you consider a sound wave to have a positive portion and a negative portion, we often refer to these as peaks and troughs. If two sources playing together don't have their positives and negatives aligned, you're going to experience negative interference, where parts of the sound, usually the low end, are reduced. When we have two signals whose positive and negative phases are aligned, we get constructive interference, resulting in an amplification of the sound. In its most extreme form, two identical signals that are completely out of phase—180° out of phase, as we would say—result in no sound at all. Although it's rare to have this in a real-world scenario, what we can experience are two signals that are slightly out of phase. That is to say, the positive and negative parts of the waves are not quite aligned, and this is where we get destructive interference at certain frequencies. This can happen when we have two microphones on the same source or perhaps a mic and a DI on a bass, even two layered kick samples that don't perfectly line up. Check out this kick drum. On its own, it sounds good with a decent amount of low end, but when I introduce another sample on top that is slightly out of phase, we experience a loss in the low end. We can see this within the phase module when I set the display to show both signals combined. As I ask the module to learn the delay time, one sample will be moving into phase. It goes from a smaller reading to a larger one as the samples get more in phase and the frequencies reinforce. Let me show you how to do that for yourself in the phase module. Multitrack drum recordings are a prime example of when phase is going to affect the sound. Signals reaching microphones at different times can cause phase inconsistencies and loss in definition and bottom end. If I zoom in on the waveform of these two overhead tracks, you can see that the waveforms are misaligned. One has a peak while the other has a trough at the same time, and this is going to cause issues. To correct this, I'm going to load an instance of Neutron 5's phase module onto the first overhead track and just explore the features a little before we tend to the job at hand. This is a plugin that can essentially learn and fix your phase issues. We have phase rotation, delay, and an oscilloscope to view what's coming into the plugin. But we also have the ability to fix asymmetrical waveforms. Asymmetrical waveforms can occur in some instruments more than others. They're particularly common in brass instruments, sometimes synths, and also in vocals. This results in variances in the harmonic content of a source. They don't necessarily mean there's an issue with recording, but they can cause issues further down the line. If you're mixing a vocal, for example, having an asymmetrical waveform could mean that the positive side of the waveform has a greater amplitude than the negative side, which is going to cause compressors and anything else dealing with loudness-based processing to act upon a signal when it doesn't actually need to. To fix this, we can use the phase module to bring back the symmetry of a waveform. You'll see on the display a mismatch in the positive (the upper portion of the waveform) and the negative portions of the signal (the lower portion of the waveform). Once I hit learn, you'll see the waveform become far more even when comparing positive to negative. By doing this, you're not really altering the sound, but you're gaining headroom and making sure that compressors or saturators aren't working harder than they should be on a signal.
