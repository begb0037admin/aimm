I love this compressor. It just holds, just glues everything together without being too... Well, you can make it so it really pumps, but it's enough to just give it a punch. Some compressors seem to make their presence felt a bit more. You hear what it's doing, but it doesn't mess up the sort of tonal balance of the mix. I think the key for me with mixed compression is getting your attack and release settings right. Because I think a lot of people assume that because it's mixed compression and you just want to be stopping overloads that you have to have a fast attack and release. Whereas I don't see it like that at all. I just want it there to help glue and to help just the feel of the sound of a compressor on the mix. It's more about lifting some of the detail out than it is about stopping those transients. So I always have quite a slow attack, which allows me to have to keep the transients happening. Especially in the low end, you've got to make sure you don't have too hard an attack. Otherwise, you just lose all that thump in the mix. So even though you're... you can be compressing quite a bit, but with a slower attack, it's a lot more gentle and you're still getting the transients through. And that's the battle for me, is to be able to get something nice and loud, but with clear transients. And the other way people do that, obviously, and sometimes I have to do it, is by using... you can get the volume up and the detail up by using layers of distortion or layers of saturation. But I don't always like the sound of that. I actually quite like really, especially with electronic music, very clear, clean bass and clean sounds with not distortion on everything.

## Chunk 3

It needs to have some dynamic in it, and it needs... because you know, you can have a track, it comes in slamming, you're like, yeah, this is really exciting, but then it goes nowhere in the chorus because it's being held in and then you're just letting the limit to do all the work. But it makes it very difficult to compare. You start the two tracks off and you listen back and say, oh, the rough mix is way more exciting. But it's not overall, because it's not going to translate to radio, because radio is going to turn that down anyway, and the whole thing is going to sound squashed and flat. Luckily, this came in at like a totally sensible level, this rough mix. But it does cause me problems when rough mixes come in to such a crazy loud degree that you think, well, I can't compete to that. So I usually have to write quite a long email explaining that this mix is going to be quieter than your rough mix. So you're going to need to turn your rough mix down 4 dB to have a comparative list as to what the differences really are. Bear in mind that it's going to be turned down mastering anyway, because you're way over the LUFS limit or Spotify would turn it down or whatever it is. So I generally aim for handing in a mix when I've got my LUFS meter on as somewhere between minus 11, minus 10, minus 9 maybe. And it also gives the mastering engineer somewhere to go as well, because otherwise, if I'm hammering it way too loud this end, then they may need to bring it down in the context of an album. And it gives them a bit of a nightmare to do. So at some point, it's got to hit the sensible level in terms of loudness. But I do do my final tweaks and adjustments with that limiter on because I want to know that it can go through limiting and come out the other side sounding good. I don't want it to, that once you put limiting on, it becomes a massive shock at mastering. It's like, well, okay, well, what's this done to the track? I'd rather have more of a shock the other way around. We take the limiter off and say, okay, well, then so I'm quite right now. That's a bit more easy to deal with than the other way around. So I definitely don't want a big shock of when we put limiting on that it's all going to go wrong. And sometimes I'll send both copies. This is the other thing, always send both to mastering. You send your limited version and the unlimited version, because then the mastering engineer knows what everybody signed off on, knows what everybody is approved. And sometimes mastering engineers will ask for my limiting settings so they can replicate it there so they know in the studio what's going on.
