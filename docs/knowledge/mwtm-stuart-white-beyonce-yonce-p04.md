# Yoncé — Part 4: Mix Bus Tracking vs Mixing Headroom Production Workflow Summing

Source: MWTM set record: docs/mwtm/sets/Stuart_White_Beyonce_Yonce_Mixing.md (local recording, no public URL)
Channel: Mix With The Masters

## Chunk 1

Let me go back a little bit and talk about the fact that there's pretty much nothing on this mix bus. I tend to do that a lot with Beyonce records for the fact that I'm tracking them and I'm thinking about it from the mentality of like an old school tracking engineer when like back in the day, everybody got sounds to tape and they tried to get the EQs and maybe the effects, all that stuff baked in on the sounds individually. So when you throw up the faders on a board, the mix pretty much plays back without anything on the stereo bus. I kind of learned that way a long time ago and I still kind of think that way when I'm tracking. I feel like in the stage of tracking, I want the song to be 90% there. Almost nothing on the mix bus. When I mix other people's projects where I didn't track it, I will use my mix template and I have a lot of saturation and different EQs and things that are all bypassed and I'll pop those in and really quickly I'll audition them and see which ones just automatically make the whole song sound better immediately. I'll have a VCA trimming all my auxes down going into that mix bus so I can play with the gain level going into it to see how hard I want to hit it. It might be a UAD Studer or a VTM Slate Tape, it might be a Phoenix, it might be a combination of those. Sometimes I'll play around with those real quick and just pop them in and just see what sounds better. But when I'm tracking, I try to not have all that stuff on the mix bus because it causes a lot of latency and I rather just work hard at getting the sounds to just all work together without a lot of that. The other advantages with artists, especially artists like Bea, she will record after a song's been mixed. Sometimes, you know, we'll have mastered something and then she decides she wants to change something, maybe re-record a part or add a part or whatever. And if I have a bunch of stuff on the mix bus, I have a lot of latency on the mix bus. Because if I record her, I could take it all off, but now the mix doesn't sound the same. So now she's doing a new part, but she's not hearing it. She's not hearing what she's adding to the thing. So as an engineer, I try to always make the process of recording as smooth and as easy as possible to the point where she can just hear it mixed all the time or as close as we possibly can to it being done. Even the first day I record a song with her, I try to mix it as close as I possibly can while she's recording. If she steps out and takes a dinner break or something and comes back, I try to use that time.

## Chunk 2

I don't take a break too. I keep working and I try to get the mix happening, maybe do some special effects and things I know she likes or whatever and start, you know, helping to finish the song and make it sound more like an end product. If I don't have anything on the mix bus, then I can trim the whole song up and down, have a lot of headroom easily. My levels are very low on my mix bus when I'm working a lot of times, but when I would print the loud pre-master at the end of the night on a rough mix, I might turn up my mix 10 to 12 dB, you know, to get it loud into my invisible limiter that I love to use. Print the loud pre-master and then once I'm done doing that, I'll bypass that invisible limiter. My headroom is balled back. If she comes in and wants to record the next day and change stuff, I'm not clipping anything. I can turn her vocal up while she's recording way louder than the track because a lot of artists want to hear themselves a lot louder than the track while they're working on a part so they can focus in on the details of what they're doing. And if I have a lot of headroom and I have it all turned down and I don't have things on the mix bus, then I can do that easily and I can move quick with her and keep her ideas flowing because I really do believe that, you know, the key to this is making all this technology transparent to their creativity, making all these knobs and all these switches not get in the way of their creative process and their flow. And so that's typically with her why I don't use a lot of stuff on the mix bus. Had I not tracked something, then I probably would use more. And then sometimes with her stuff, I still do. I might put an SSL compressor on there. I might put a tape plug in or something at some point. You know, never say never, but that's my thought process with the mix bus as her tracking engineer. When I look at this session and how simplistic it is, it just goes to show that, you know, the producers did their job on the arranging, you know, and with this one, I did help arrange it a bit and there was a lot more sounds in it and we were in crunch time mode and tried a different combination of sounds or whatever that she ended up liking and going with just to help out the process and speed it up and get it done because there was just a lot of other parts in there and we had to strip it back in, like, you know, keep it simple and maintain the focal point of what sounded the best and just building it, painting all around her.

## Chunk 3

And the sounds were good, you know, it's just when you got Justin and Timberlund and Mike Dean, like, sending you sounds, you know, it just really does help to the point where you don't need to be mixing through a lot of saturation or whatever to fatten up the sounds because the sounds already have what they need. And I'm a big, if it ain't broke, don't fix it. I keep saying that. And it's so true. And one of the arts of mixing is just knowing when it doesn't need that and when you don't need to do that technique. I know on the video I did back in 2018 with Autolux, I had two mix buses and I had tons of saturation on them. I had parallel. So I had like a mix bus, another mix bus and parallels for both of them. And the reason I did that is because there was a different section that came later in that song. It was a completely different drum sound and it wasn't reacting to the settings of the first mix bus. So to make it work quickly, I just duplicated the mix bus and altered it in a way where it did work and it felt right and it felt cohesive and musical. And I'm not afraid to do that. And that's the beauty of Pro Tools because you can make as many as you want if you need to. I did it for a reason and that's why this track came out so simple. It's because everything is done with intention for a reason. The song came together without having to have a lot of fancy stuff on the mix bus or whatever. Also, I wanted to have that low end really huge. And if I was compressing the mix bus too much, it would choke that probably. And so I really wanted to shape the low end in a way and combine it with the vocals where the low end is not ducking the vocals and the vocals aren't ducking the low end and it's all working independently. So that's another reason why I didn't do a lot on the mix bus. But, you know, and again, it's like this is all mixed in the box. You know, some people like, oh, you might say, hey, why didn't you, why wouldn't you try analog something to see if it improves? And again, I just felt like it sounded so good like this and the clarity and the hi-fi quality of the sounds just shows through in the box quite well. Because in the box, it's not adding anything. It just is preserving what's there. Sometimes a lot of people might go down the rabbit hole of thinking they need an SSL or like all these summing buses and all this stuff to make great mixes because their favorite mixers might use all that stuff. But those mixers that use all that stuff that are great, they would do an equally great mix in the box probably.

## Chunk 4

And it's in the same way like heroes of mine like Chad Blake, you know, went from analog into digital and managed to create the sound that he hears in his head from using the tools in digital or whatever just shows that you can do it any way you want. And it's just knowing your tools and knowing when and when not to do things, when something's good, when to leave it alone and when not to. But yeah, this one came together very simply. And sometimes I wish they all could come together this simply, but it's a need to basis. You know, don't do something because it's your chain. You know, don't, you know, feel like you have to force something into your chain necessarily. Try to see if it's cool without it and then work from there is what I would say. And don't be afraid to use the basic stock plugins too because they can work fine. People have commented about it to me for a long time as far as, you know, the low end and how it makes speakers move and how it shows off a speaker's ability to show low end. So I hope you got something out of it. I got something out of looking at it again. I haven't looked at it in 10 years. I hope it was helpful.
