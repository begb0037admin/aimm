# Care — Part 4: Bussing System Filter Automation Gain Staging Mix Headroom Basic Principles

Source: MWTM set record: docs/mwtm/sets/Leslie_Brathwaite_IV_Jay_Care_Mixing.md (local recording, no public URL)
Channel: Mix With The Masters

## Chunk 1

One of the interesting things about my busing system, especially when it comes to instruments, I tend not to have individual buses for groups of instruments. A lot of mixes or sessions that I get or other mixers will tend to mix that way. I tend not to do that. I like to just have all the instruments from the 808 all the way down through the keys to the whatever. I have all of these bus to one instrument bus. It's just the way my mind works. For me, if I have a drum bus and like a guitar bus and a keyboards bus, what that does is it gives me the opportunity to start second guessing things. It's easier for me to go, hmm, I wonder if I need to turn the drums up. If I have a drum dedicated bus where I can only affect the drums this way, I'm forced to commit to a blend and to have things work together in a certain way. And that's just, again, how my mind works. I like doing it this way where I don't give myself all that opportunity to go back and second guess. Oh, maybe I should turn up the guitars as a group. Maybe I should turn up the drums as a group. I like the initial vibe and feel that I come up with as I move through the song. I think that part is important of building the vibe. I like the decisions I make along the way. And I rarely want to go back and turn up the drums as a whole. I feel like the decision I made to have the keyboards or the strings sit in a certain place based on where the drums were, I like to honor those initial instinct decisions. Those usually your first, you know, I've seen it all the time and this just doesn't apply just to mixers. I see it with producers, with songwriters where the first iteration of that thing, like with songwriters, the first melody they usually come up with and then they might make some changes and then they usually revert to the first idea that they had. And so I like to just honor that thought process with my buses. I usually don't have a drum bus or this bus or that bus. I just every instrument to one bus. A lot of times when all my instruments are bused to an aux before they go to my main aux, what I find useful with that is on that aux, I can do things like say I wanted to filter out an entire section of the music, then I can put the filter on the instrument aux and then I can automate the filter. I can automate when it starts, when it ends, that kind of thing and make the whole track filtered out in a certain section. You know, I do that on different records sometimes like with Uzi Vert or with Drake or, you know, other people who require that kind of thing.

## Chunk 2

So that's a handy thing to have the instrument bus. On this particular song, I didn't need to do that. So that bus is just at zero and then feeding my main bus. And then on my main bus, like I said, I don't put anything. Right here, what you see is the FGX, the Slate Digital FGX. And this just helps to where when I'm printing this for a client for them to listen to, I want it to be at a certain level because nine times out of 10, the client is comparing my mix to something that's already out and mastered. So I want to give my mix a fair shot when they're comparing it to other things that are released. So that's why I have this on the main bus. But when I print the mix for mastering, I disengage this. It's usually bypassed. So nothing on the instrument bus, nothing on the main bus, nothing on the vocal bus. That's just how I roll. And a lot of those buses are really just helpful functions in case I need to go back and do something overall. Like if the comment that comes back from the mix is, you know what? And I've had this a lot. You know what? I love the mix. Do me one favor. All the vocals, bump them up a dB or two. Then I can just come up here to my vocal bus where all the vocals are running through this bus and I can just go like this and then print again. And they're usually happy. So a lot of times the buses aren't always there because something's happening on them, a lot of times the buses are there strategically placed. So they are a function of being able to come back and make a quick change. And that usually is very helpful. A lot of the comments I get back, like I said, why it's important to always have like a main vocal bus and a main instrument bus is because a lot of the comments I get are either A, on the vocal side, hey, can you just turn up all the vocals? And B, on the instrument side, hey, I want to put a filter in the bridge. Can we filter out the whole bridge? Well, I already have the bus. I just throw the filter on there and automate it. So my goal as I mix records, I'm always thinking about the mastering process. So my goal is to usually leave about three to six dB of headroom for the mastering engineer to be able to do his thing. What that means is simply just not slamming the stereo bus so that if he does want to do some multiband compression or if he wants to add a little oomph to the overall mix or whatever the case may be, he has some headroom for that signal to kind of play with. If the signal is already slamming the stereo bus, he doesn't have much to work with.

## Chunk 3

So I like to leave them three to six dB to work with. There's no specific formula or number. I just like to leave them a little room and you can kind of gauge where you're at as far as how much room you're leaving them. So I do respect the fact that another process is happening and I like to honor that. And every now and then I check in with the mastering engineers, especially the ones I use the most like Colin Leonard, you know, Dave Kutch, Chris Athens, all those guys. I check in with them every now and then and say, hey, how's my mixes been sounding? We good? They've been okay? Cool. In printing mixes, especially considering mastering, one of the techniques I gravitate to the most is if I feel like the overall level is too loud or want to leave a little more headroom. Sometimes it's as simple as just turning down the overall master fader and leaving some headroom as you print the track. Now, if the distortion or the clipping or the the slamming is happening before, then I would look into maybe turning down the instruments as a group and the vocals as a group. I rarely look at clip gaining as that function because what happens when you clip gain is now you're affecting what's feeding the plugins and you're affecting how much compression is getting pressed on certain buses and that. So when you get into that, now you're messing with your gain structure. So it's important. That's one of the things I'd stress on in mixing is it's important to have a good sense of your gain structure from the beginning. It's important to understand this needs to be at this level to have the maximum impact, but still leave room for this stuff. Gain staging is so important in mixing. So that's one of the things I stress. With gain staging, so even though I start with vocals first, when I'm doing vocals and starting with vocals first, I'm not thinking of levels as far as relative to the entire mix. I'm just dealing with the sonics of the vocals. I'm cleaning things up. I'm determining the EQs, how I want the vocals to feel, how I want them to feel in relation to each other, but I'm not thinking of levels just yet as far as overall levels. So it's not like I'm mixing the vocals and then getting them to a certain level and then bringing in the music to match the level I have the vocals. It's actually the other way around. I start with the vocals. I do cleanup. I do EQing, tuning, lead relative to backgrounds. And even that is not a thing that I'm stuck on at that point. I'm just trying to make sure they sound how I want them to sound, but I'm not concerned with levels. I'm not really concerned with the level of the lead concern, you know, as opposed to the level of the background.

## Chunk 4

I'm not concerned with those levels yet. All I'm concerned with is the sound and the shape of the vocals. And then I do the music. And when I start on the kicks and the bass, that's the thing where I know, okay, these need to hit, these need to come in hitting that around minus 12, minus six, so that everything else I build on top of that, I know where I'm going to end up on the meters. And I know how much I'm going to pack on on top of that. So by the time I finish the music, to the point like where I'm at now, where I'm finished blending the music, I have a pretty good sense of where the meters are hitting, where the mix is hitting, and where it's going to go to once I start bringing the vocals in. But the levels of the vocals aren't even determined yet, just the shape and the sound of them. The levels are now of the vocals are now going to be based on where the music sits. The music is the base for how I determine how hot the mix is going to hit or how loud the levels are going to be. And then the vocals now fit into that picture. Mixing is less of a science and more of an art. And I really take that comparison seriously. I don't like to make this rocket science. I don't like to get into ghost reverbs and creating space with a reverb on the hi-hat and putting a small reverb on the bass and da-da-da. Not insulting anyone that does because your genre or your mind state may call for that. The genre of music that I usually work in, hip-hop, trap, R&B, pop, it doesn't really call for a lot of reverb on hi-hats or reverb on guitars or reverbs on pianos or trying to create space. Space isn't something that really is applicable to this genre, especially with hip-hop. It's about hit. It's about, is it banging? Is it pumping? Is it knocking? Is it giving you that feel, the emotion? Is all that there? And so a lot of times, again, it's more of an art than it is a science. It's more of an art of how is this making me feel? What's the feel of it? And that's the thing that determines how I move. I don't try to add things for the sake of adding things. The way I mix, I mix in a very subtractive state. So my mind state is actually working the other way, which is what can I take away first? What don't I like about this sound? But I'm not in the mind frame of, oh, you have to add plugins and you have to do this and you have to do that. And I hate to call it misconceptions, but those are some of the things about mixing that make people feel like it's more of a science and they get more in their head and they feel like I should be doing more and that or that.

## Chunk 5

And when I show people my sessions and they see these are the plugins that I have on this session, it's really not a lot of plugins. And there are some instruments that don't have any plugins on them. You don't always have to put a plugin on something just to say you mixed a song. I always like to revert to the basic principles, which is levels, panning, just making it feel good. So sometimes I will use like some type of stereo widener. If I do use them, mostly it's like on things like strings or vibey instruments where I try to spread things out just a bit to give it more of a stereo to take up more of the stereo space that happens on the outer rim.
