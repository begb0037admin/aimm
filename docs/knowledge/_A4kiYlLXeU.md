Before I show you the trick, I need to give you a super quick explanation of how Spotify or any streaming service actually measures your song's loudness. Because once you get this, the whole thing will click. You probably heard the term LUFS thrown around. LUFS stands for loudness units full scale. All it really means is how loud does your song actually sound to the human ear, on average, over the entire track. It's not measuring your loudest moments, it's measuring the whole picture of the song. And you might have heard people say, "Just master to -14 LUFS and you're good." But, that's not how the real world works. Pull up any major release right now, hip-hop, pop, EDM, those masters are sitting at -7, maybe -8, possibly even -6 LUFS. Engineers are still mastering loud. Not only cuz the mixing and mastering engineers are getting loud roughs, but our ears are used to the excitement from overly limited music. So, the goal is to get your master as loud and competitive as possible, and then make sure Spotify is measuring it as favorably as it can. And that's where the LUFS gate comes in. Here's the thing most people don't know. The way LUFS is calculated, there is a built-in gate in the measurement. If a section of your song is too quiet, soft intro, or breakdown, or the fade out, the section gets excluded from the loudness calculation entirely. It just doesn't count. So, take a hard-hitting hip-hop track that opens with 8 seconds of a quiet intro. Spotify's meter is running the whole time, but that intro is getting thrown out of the measurement. When those quiet sections get excluded, your integrated LUFS reading actually ends up higher than it should be. Those soft moments are secretly inflating your number, and Spotify is using the inflated number to decide how loud to play your song. So, instead of hammering your limiter harder and wrecking your dynamics, you find those sections falling below the gate, bring them up just enough to get counted, and your integrated LUFS becomes more accurate and more competitive.
