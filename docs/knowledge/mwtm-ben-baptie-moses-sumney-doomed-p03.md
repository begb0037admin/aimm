## Chunk 1

Those initial reverbs that I used were really, really beneficial. They kind of give me a place that I can sit within. And then you just get into the more nuanced areas. So these tracks here, these are all outboard stuff. So this top one here is a real plate. Below that is an Eventide H3000 set to Canyon 211. And then these two here are different guitar pedals doing these long reverb kind of spills. The way that I would have done these two outboard reverbs is they would have been coming off of the console. So I would have been feeding them from a large fader on the console to really kind of get my hands onto them. What they allow with this other bed as well, and you'll be able to see there's a lot of automation that's going on in these reverbs. I've got a VCA here controlled in the box ones, as well as all of this going on. On the, even though these are prints, you know, I've still continued to automate them. There's a lot of nuance that's going on with a lot of different reverbs all around this one vocal. And so what you're doing is you're pushing and pulling the vocal from being like a very, very dry in your face thing, but with this world that's sitting around it. And if you can get the balance right, you create this like really, really gentle, but controlling aspect of the song. And I think that is kind of key to how this works. I mean, you can hear that like his vocal is so gentle and these like long held notes, it's so easy for them to become lost. You know, this kind of held sustained note here, you could lose that so easily. And you want it to kind of feel like it's consistently skimming off the surface of this synth. And, you know, this kind of build. And so it's just really, really important to, if I just play just the wet aspects of the vocals. You can hear there's just so much movement. You've got stuff which has got a really quick attack. You've got stuff which has got a much slower attack. You've got aspects that are, for instance, like the really, really compressed D verb, doesn't have any attack. You've got things that have got predlay on them, which also have a quick attack. It's creating almost a rhythmical aspect that sits underneath his voice. So that his vocal will hit and then you have a progression of sounds after it. So that your ear isn't just focusing on this very one-dimensional thing. It creates this whole thing behind it. Which, you know, does take time. It takes a lot of patience to get to that area. Because you can really feel that it works, for instance, by itself like this. And then as soon as you're putting everything else in with it, it can start to overpower.

## Chunk 2

You can start, you know, because you don't ever really want to hear the reverbs either. You want to feel them. And I think getting that balance was the thing I definitely spent the most time on with the mix. Because his vocal, to some degree, will always feel dry. It will always feel like he's just talking to you. But the space in which you've created it is a space that you are both within. And it's those kind of moments that really do take a lot of patience within a mix. And it's something that when I'm mixing personally, I, the beginning of the day, I will loop around to sections of songs and stuff like that. And, you know, definitely at the beginning of this mix, it would be like sitting on this first verse into the second verse. Just getting the kind of foundation of the song right. Over and over and over and over and over. By the end of the day, and by the end of this mix, I had to play the song from front to back every single time. Because by this stage, you might be able to just play this section and it might feel fine. But you need it to come from here. This here is where you set the markers of the song. This is where you propose what is about to happen to the listener. And if this bit is slightly overcooked, by the time you get to this point, it's just going to be chaos for the listener. They won't know where they're at and it will be too much. You won't have this natural kind of progression. And I think even though, you know, it's very, very easy to, you know, always say like, a harder mix has got to be something that's got loads and loads of stuff in it. I actually don't think that's the case. I think the hardest, you know, the thing that can be so hard in a mix and the thing that can take a lot of time is the density of a mix. It's what emotionally is having to happen to keep someone engaged, to give them a kind of the story that is unfolding within the song in both a natural way, but also in a way that is like the most heightened version of what it can be. You know, this song is talking about if you don't have love, are you doomed? Like that's a heavy subject. How do you do that? So that it both keeps a listener to the point where they are able to see it within themselves, but also doesn't make people just be like, wow, this is really hard to listen to. I don't actually want to listen to this. It's like you have to retain beauty, but you also have to intensify it. So that it's an incredibly moving passage of music. And I think within that, that is where making decisions on some things are really, really important, but also just going back and not being afraid to really try like big changes on other things is a very important part of it.

## Chunk 3
