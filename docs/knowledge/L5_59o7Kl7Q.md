I know a lot of you will have been told to mono the low end of your songs, but where did this thinking originate from, and is it still relevant today? These are big questions with substantial implications for how we engineer our music. And I, for one, am super curious. Let's dive in and get some answers. Recommendations for a fully mono low end originated in vinyl mastering, where excessive bass level or side channel low-frequency information could cause problems. The mono bass argument was further promoted by the fact that PA rigs typically have their subs configured in mono. And we're often told that the low end of our songs will sound more powerful, tighter, and more consistent if we just mono it. The process of monoing the low end involved using a high-pass filter on the side channel to filter out low-frequency content. This is sometimes referred to as an elliptical filter. For the purposes of this video, I'm going to refer to that process as hard monoing the low end. Hard monoing the low end has several potential downsides, though. First is an increase in peak level. Second is phase smearing. If you're using a nonlinear phase filter on the side channel, then you can smear the phase relationship between mid and side, right in the critical bass region where phase coherence is so important. The third is that you can experience level loss. If you remove information in the side channel, then overall bass level can drop. Fourth is you can have timbral changes. If there's information that exists only in the side channel—which is otherwise known as antiphase content—by removing that, you're actually removing detail and not just level. And the fifth and final thing is you can experience a loss of envelopment. There is perceivable spaciousness in bass, sometimes called basiousness. And when you strip that out, you lose that, which is potentially something special and interesting in your mix. Assuming the vast majority of you are not making music that's ever going to find its way onto vinyl, is the hard mono approach still valid? Without a doubt, the bottom end of your song needs to be robust, and you need to make sure that it's going to translate well to all of the various playback destinations that your listeners might be using. But the hard mono approach might be a bit too heavy-handed for modern music, especially considering the percentage of people that are listening on headphones and full stereo playback systems. The genre of music where people are told the most strongly to mono the low end, I think, is electronic music—ostensibly because all PAs have the subs configured in mono, and it'll just sound better that way. If you're wondering why these artists and engineers are leaving some width in the bass and subfrequency ranges, you might be interested in some of the research on the matter. It was previously thought that we couldn't perceive directionality or width in low-frequency ranges, but that's been discovered to be untrue. Newer research supports that the human auditory system is capable of both spatially locating and perceiving width in low-frequency ranges. This paper by David Griesinger describes the exact mechanism of spatial detection at low frequencies. Stereo bass isn't just about our ability to perceive the apparent location or direction of a sound or instrument—it's also about a sense of spaciousness or dimension. So maybe let's not call it stereo bass anymore. I prefer a term coined by Todd Welti, an acoustician at Harman: basiousness. Basiousness is a sense of envelopment, immersiveness, dimension, or spaciousness at low frequencies. Not only can you perceive basiousness, there's research which indicates that listeners actually prefer it. There's a Journal of the Audio Engineering Society paper called “Nearfield Crosstalk Increases Listener Preference for Headphone-Reproduced Stereophonic Imagery.” That's a mouthful.
